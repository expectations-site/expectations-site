The website is done. That's the easy part.

Most clothing brands fail not because of bad websites or bad products —
they fail because of 4 things:

1. No one knows they exist

# PART 1: YOUR PRODUCT MUST BE UNDENIABLE

## This comes before everything else.

Before you spend a dollar on marketing, you need honest answers to:

**Does the quality match the price?**
- Order samples from your manufacturer before the first drop
- Wear the hoodie for a week. Wash it 3 times. Does it still look right?
- If you wouldn't pay $89 for it, your customer won't either

**Is it actually different?**
- Why EXPECTATIONS over Supreme, Palace, or the 1,000 other streetwear brands?
- What's the one thing about your aesthetic that no one else does?
- Your answer to this question becomes every caption, every shoot, every story

**Is the fit right?**
- Bad fit kills reviews and inflates returns
- Find 5–10 people of different body types to try the samples
- Adjust patterns before the first production run

**The rule:** Ship nothing you wouldn't proudly wear in public yourself.

### Pillars (rotate through these):
1. **Product** — showcasing pieces in real environments
2. **Process** — behind the scenes: packing orders, factory visits, design sketches
3. **Culture** — the music, people, places, ideas that EXPECTATIONS lives inside
4. **Community** — reposting customers wearing the brand, DMs, reviews
5. **Story** — founder content, journey, milestones, struggles

### Formats that work for streetwear right now:
- TikTok: pack-with-me videos, unboxing, try-ons, behind the scenes
- Instagram Reels: outfit styling, lookbook clips, drop previews
- Instagram Grid: editorial photography, brand world building
- Instagram Stories: polls, "which colour?", shipping updates, flash sales

### Volume:
- TikTok: 1–2 videos per day minimum to start
- Instagram Reels: 4–5 per week
- Stories: daily

### The hardest truth:
The first 90 days, you will post to almost no one.
You post anyway. Every day. Without exception.

## 3.2 — Photography

**This is the single biggest lever you have.**

Bad photography kills good product.
Great photography sells average product.

### What you need for launch:
- **Lookbook shoot:** 1 full day, 2–3 models, 3–4 locations
- Shots: full body, mid-shot, detail close-ups, lifestyle candid
- Lighting: natural light > studio for streetwear
- Editing: consistent tones across all images (pick a preset and stick to it)

### Who to shoot with:
- Find a local photographer with a strong editorial portfolio
- Don't use the same photographer everyone uses — find someone hungry
- Pay fairly. A bad shoot is more expensive than a good one.
- Budget: $500–$1,500 for a proper launch shoot

### Model casting:
- Use real people from your community
- Diversity of body types and skin tones — your brand is for everyone
- People who actually wear streetwear, not catalogue models

## 3.3 — Influencer Strategy

### The mistake everyone makes:
Paying big influencers for one post that nobody remembers.

### What actually works:
**Micro-influencers (5K–50K followers)**
- Higher engagement than mega-influencers
- Niche audiences who actually buy streetwear
- Will often post for free product at the start
- Build real relationships with 10–20 of them

**How to approach:**
- DM: "I've been following your content for a while. We're launching EXPECTATIONS — [short story]. I'd love to send you a piece, no strings attached."
- Don't make it transactional upfront
- Send the best piece you have, not the cheapest

**Community seeding:**
Before you launch publicly, send product to:
- 20 people in your local scene who are genuinely respected
- People who won't just post for free product — people who'll actually wear it
- When 20 people are wearing your brand before launch day, you have social proof

## 3.4 — Drop Strategy

### Don't open a store and leave it open forever.

The brands that create urgency win.

**The Drop Model:**
- Limited quantities released at specific times
- Announced 1–2 weeks in advance
- Countdown, teasers, behind-the-scenes leading up
- Sells out = proof of demand = pressure for the next drop

**Drop cadence for year 1:**
- Drop 1: Core pieces (3–5 items) — test what sells
- Drop 2: Build on what sold in Drop 1 — new colourways or styles
- Drop 3: Introduce one new category
- Seasonal: align drops with spring and autumn (when people buy clothes)

**Waitlist:**
Add an email waitlist to the site (already have email capture).
"Join the waitlist for Drop 2" converts much better than "subscribe."

## 3.5 — Paid Advertising

**Don't run ads until you have proof of concept.**
First, validate with organic. When an organic post gets strong engagement,
then put $20–$50 behind it as an ad.

When you're ready:

**Meta Ads (Instagram/Facebook):**
- Start with retargeting: people who visited the site but didn't buy
- Lookalike audiences: find people who look like your existing customers
- Budget: $20–$50/day to start, scale what works

**TikTok Ads:**
- Spark Ads: boost your best organic TikToks as ads
- Very cost-effective for streetwear right now

**Google Shopping:**
- Set up Google Merchant Center with your product catalog
- Captures people actively searching for what you sell

# PART 4: THE BUSINESS ENGINE

## 4.1 — Unit Economics (Know Your Numbers)

Before every drop, know this:

```
Revenue needed to break even = Fixed costs / Gross margin %

Example:
Fixed costs (manufacturing run + shipping): $3,500
Average sale price: $89
Average cost per unit (manufacturing + packaging + shipping): $32
Gross margin: ($89 - $32) / $89 = 64%

Break-even units: $3,500 / $57 = 62 units

You need to sell 62 pieces to break even.
If your drop has 100 pieces, you need a 62% sell-through rate.
If you don't believe you can hit that, either reduce the run or don't produce.
```

### Know before every production run:
- Minimum viable order quantity from manufacturer
- Total landed cost per unit (manufacturing + shipping to you)
- Break-even sell-through rate
- Target margin at full sell-through

---

## 4.2 — Cash Flow

**This kills more brands than anything else.**

Clothing requires cash upfront for production.
Revenue comes weeks or months later.

### How to manage it:
- **Pre-orders:** Sell before you manufacture. Collect payment, then place the production order. Reduces risk to near zero. Scuffers and many other brands do this.
- **Start small:** Better to sell out of 50 units than be stuck with 500
- **Don't reinvest 100%:** Keep 3 months of operating costs in reserve
- **Payment terms:** Negotiate with your manufacturer — 50% upfront, 50% on delivery

### Pre-order model:
"Drop 1 — Pre-order open for 7 days. Ships in 4 weeks."
You know your demand before you spend a dollar on manufacturing.

---

## 4.3 — Pricing Strategy

### Common mistake: pricing too low to "be accessible"

Low prices signal low quality in fashion. They also leave no room for:
- Discounts and sales
- Influencer gifting
- Returns and exchanges
- Paid advertising
- Your own salary

### Pricing framework:
```
Base price = (Manufacturing cost × 5) minimum for DTC
$28 cost → $140 price? That's where luxury streetwear lives.
$28 cost → $89 price? Accessible premium. Where EXPECTATIONS is now.

Make sure you can still run a 20% off sale and be profitable.
```

### Sales strategy:
- Never discount new stock at full price
- End-of-season clearance: sell old stock at 30–40% off
- Black Friday: planned in advance, one week only
- Never train your customer to wait for sales

**Getting a customer is expensive. Keeping one is nearly free.**

# PART 5: OPERATIONS

## 5.1 — Your Manufacturer Relationship

This is a business partnership. Treat it like one.

- Visit the factory if possible — even once builds enormous trust
- Communicate clearly and in writing — use the portal you have
- Pay on time, every time
- Give accurate demand forecasts — don't over-promise orders
- Have a backup manufacturer identified before you need one

**Quality control checklist for every production run:**
- [ ] Request pre-production sample for approval
- [ ] Check stitching, seams, zips, prints before approving bulk
- [ ] Order 5% extra units to cover defects
- [ ] Inspect delivery before accepting — document any faults immediately

---

## 5.2 — Inventory Management

### The two inventory disasters:
1. **Stockouts:** You sell out too fast, lose revenue, frustrate customers
2. **Dead stock:** You're left with unsold inventory tying up cash

### How to avoid both:
- Start with conservative quantities — better to sell out
- Track sell-through rate per product, per colourway, per size
- Your analytics dashboard shows this — use it after every drop
- Reorder when stock hits 20% remaining, not when it hits zero
- Identify your "hero products" (the Archive Hoodie is likely one) and always have them in stock

---

## 5.3 — Customer Service

**One bad experience shared publicly can undo months of brand building.**

### Standards:
- Respond to every enquiry within 24 hours (48h maximum)
- When something goes wrong, fix it immediately and over-deliver
- If a customer receives a faulty item, replace it before asking for the return
- Never argue with a customer publicly — take it to DMs

### Tools:
- Set up a dedicated support email: support@expectations.co
- Use Missive or Zendesk to manage when volume increases
- Create a FAQ page to reduce repetitive enquiries (I can build this)

# PART 6: THE LONG GAME

## Year 1: Survive and learn
- Validate product-market fit
- Build your community from 0 to 1,000 real followers
- Execute 2–3 drops, learn what sells
- Break even or achieve small profit
- Build systems so the business doesn't depend solely on you every day

## Year 2: Scale what works
- Double down on the products and channels that drove sales in Year 1
- Invest in better photography and campaign production
- Explore wholesale to select boutiques (adds credibility)
- Hire a part-time social media person or photographer
- Launch loyalty programme

## Year 3: Brand, not just product
- Collaborate with other brands, artists, or musicians
- Pop-up events and activations
- Expand to new product categories based on data
- Consider stockists in key cities (Tokyo, Paris, London, Lagos, NYC)
- The brand has a clear point of view that people talk about

# WHAT SEPARATES BRANDS THAT LAST

The brands that survive 5 years all share these traits:

**1. The founder is also the storyteller**
They show up personally. People know who's behind the brand.

**2. They obsess over the community, not the follower count**
1,000 real supporters who wear the brand > 100,000 passive followers.

**3. They ship product they're proud of**
They never compromise quality to save money.

**4. They know their numbers cold**
They never produce what they can't sell.

**5. They're patient**
Every brand you admire today looked exactly like yours does in year one.
They just didn't stop.
